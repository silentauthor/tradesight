# Trading Psychology — Mark Douglas, Jack Schwager, and Livermore Maxims

## Trading in the Zone — Mark Douglas

**The Five Fundamental Truths**: (1) anything can happen; (2) you don't need to know what happens next to make money; (3) there is a random distribution between wins and losses for any given edge; (4) an edge is only a higher probability of one outcome over another, not a certainty; (5) every moment in the market is unique.

**Thinking in probabilities**: treat each trade as one instance in a large statistical series where any single outcome is unknowable, but an edge plays out reliably over enough trades (Douglas cites roughly 20 trades as a minimum sample to evaluate an edge). A trader who has internalized this doesn't need to be right on any one trade and feels no real emotional difference between a winner and a loser.

**Seven Principles of Consistency** (a mantra for internalizing this mindset): objectively identify your edges; predefine the risk of every trade; fully accept that risk or let go of the trade; act on your edges without hesitation; pay yourself as the market makes money available; continually monitor your own susceptibility to error; understand that these principles must never be violated.

**Self-sabotage**: the market is neutral — fear, pain, and euphoria are self-generated from the trader's own beliefs, not caused by the market. Unresolved beliefs about deservingness, the need to be right, or revenge against a prior loss are described as the drivers of destructive trading patterns that persist even when a trader intellectually "knows better." Taking full personal responsibility for every outcome, rather than blaming the market, is framed as the non-negotiable starting point for change.

**Trader development stages**: mechanical (rigid rule-following to build self-trust), subjective (a dangerous transitional stage where personal judgment starts to override discipline), intuitive (mastery, reached only after fully internalizing the first two stages).

## Market Wizards — Jack Schwager (cross-trader consensus)

The most repeated rule across the interviewed traders: **cut your losses short and let your profits run** — described as the hardest rule to actually follow despite near-universal agreement on it.

- Ed Seykota: the elements of good trading are cutting losses, cutting losses, and cutting losses; keep bets small; follow the rules without question, but know when to break them.
- Bruce Kovner: never risk a large amount of money for reasons you don't understand; always know your exit before entering; keep single-idea risk to a small, fixed percentage of equity.
- Larry Hite: never risk more than 1% of total equity on any trade; risk management, not being right, is the entire game; never trade counter to the system's established trend.
- Paul Tudor Jones: play great defense, not great offense; never average into a loser; decrease size when trading poorly, increase it when trading well; the moment you feel you're very good is the moment you're finished.
- Marty Schwartz: know your "uncle point" (maximum acceptable loss) before every trade; the biggest losses tend to follow the biggest wins, so take a break after a strong run; money management is repeated three times for emphasis.
- Van Tharp's synthesis of interviewing many top traders: shared beliefs included that money itself isn't the point, it's acceptable to lose on any given trade, and trading is approached like a game that's mentally "won" before it even starts; the four main psychological obstacles for most traders are poor risk handling, stress-driven simplistic decisions, internal conflict between conscious goals and unconscious habits, and lack of emotional control.

## Livermore maxims (Reminiscences of a Stock Operator)

Note: the copy of this book in the original library was only a 15-page preview (cover, table of contents, foreword) and did not contain the actual chapters. The maxims below are well-established, widely-cited attributions to Livermore/Lefèvre from general knowledge, not verified against a full-text extraction of this specific copy:

- "The big money is not in the buying and the selling, but in the waiting" — patience holding a correctly-timed position is where the real profit is made.
- "Don't fight the tape" — the trend, not personal opinion, is what matters.
- Pyramid into winning positions; never average down into a losing one.
- Wait for a decisive, confirmed move rather than trying to guess exact tops and bottoms.
- Cut losses immediately and without hesitation.
