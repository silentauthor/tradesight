# Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets — John J. Murphy

Notes here focus on what goes beyond generic TA already covered elsewhere.

## Point & Figure charting

Box size is chosen per instrument/price level and trades signal-frequency against reliability. The "3-box reversal" is the dominant modern convention — a column only reverses direction after price moves 3 full boxes the other way (1-box for very short-term sensitivity, 5-box for heavier filtering). Distinct measuring technique: a **horizontal count** multiplies the box-width of a congestion base by the box size and the reversal number to get a price target; a **vertical count** instead measures a single strong column's length and projects a similar move from a breakout.

## Elliott Wave

Pattern matters more than ratio, which matters more than time. Hard rules: wave 2 never retraces more than 100% of wave 1; wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1/3/5; wave 4 never overlaps wave 1's price territory; corrections always occur in three waves; if wave 2 is a sharp correction, wave 4 tends to be sideways/flat (alternation). Fibonacci relationships: wave 2 commonly retraces 38%, 50%, or 62% of wave 1; wave 3 often extends to 1.618× wave 1's length; Fibonacci numbers are also applied to time (measuring days/weeks/months between highs and lows). A channel is drawn through waves 2 and 4 with a parallel line through the top of wave 3; wave 5 often terminates at or slightly beyond that parallel line.

## Intermarket analysis

A four-market chain: **dollar vs. commodities** (inverse — a rising dollar is disinflationary and bearish for most commodities; gold acts as a leading indicator for commodities generally); **commodities vs. bonds** (inverse — rising commodities/inflation pressure pushes bond prices down); **bonds vs. stocks** (normally *positive* — rising bond prices/falling yields is bullish for equities); with an explicit **deflation exception** where bonds and stocks decouple, both moving in the same direction as each other rather than the normal positive correlation (cited with the 1997–98 Asian crisis). Utility stocks are described as a leading indicator for Treasury bond futures; gold mining shares lead the gold price itself. Sector rotation follows this chain: strong bonds/weak commodities favor rate-sensitive sectors (utilities, financials, staples); the reverse favors inflation-sensitive sectors (energy, materials, cyclicals). A rising dollar favors domestically-focused small caps over multinational large caps (whose overseas earnings get squeezed), and vice versa.

## Moving average refinements

Cycle-based period selection ("harmonic principle" — neighboring cycles relate by a factor of ~2): the ~20-21 trading-day monthly cycle explains the popularity of 5/10/20/40-day moving averages. Fibonacci-derived periods (13, 21, 34, 55) are also commonly used, with 21-day and 13-week specifically called out as proven-useful. A well-known triple-crossover system: the **4-9-18 day** system (R.C. Allen) — a buy *alert* fires when the 4-day crosses above both the 9- and 18-day averages; a *confirmed* buy needs the 9-day to also cross above the 18-day. The **4-Week Rule** (a Donchian-style channel breakout): buy on a close above the highest high of the preceding 4 full weeks, sell on a close below the lowest low of the preceding 4 weeks — shortened to 1–2 weeks for more sensitivity, or lengthened to 8 weeks to reduce whipsaw.

## Volume & open interest

A four-way matrix ties price/volume/open-interest together: rising price + rising volume + rising OI = strong (genuine new buying); rising price + falling volume/OI = weak (short-covering, not real demand); the mirror applies on the downside. A buildup of open interest during a sideways range predicts a *more intense* eventual breakout, since more traders end up trapped on the wrong side. The Commitments of Traders report's core contrarian rule: commercials (large hedgers) are usually right at turning points; large speculators and small traders are usually wrong.

## Oscillator thresholds (more precise than generic 70/30)

RSI: standard 14-period; overbought/oversold bands shift from 70/30 to **80/20** in strong bull/bear trends respectively; 50 is the RSI midpoint and can itself act as support/resistance. Stochastics: standard 14-period, but overbought/oversold thresholds are **80/20**, not 70/30. CCI: default 20-day period, overbought/oversold at **±100** (an unbounded scale, unlike RSI/Stochastics). MACD: standard parameters are **12, 26, 9**. Bullish Consensus/contrary-opinion sentiment: above ~75% bullish flags a possible top, below ~25% flags a possible bottom. ADX: rising above roughly 40 then turning down signals an approaching trading range; turning up from below roughly 20 signals a trend resuming — Wilder's own estimate is that markets trend only about **30% of the time**.

## Combining signals without contradiction

Use the weekly chart to establish direction and only take a daily signal that agrees with it. Use ADX to choose the toolset: trend-following tools (moving averages, breakout systems) when ADX is rising/high; oscillators (RSI, Stochastics, CCI) when ADX is falling/low, since trend-following systems whipsaw badly in the ~70% of the time markets are non-trending. Oscillator extremes are unreliable early in a fresh strong trend and become more reliable as a move matures. Breadth confirmation (advance-decline line, new highs/lows, up/down volume) and Dow Theory's requirement that multiple averages confirm each other are both cited as explicit multi-indicator confluence checks, not single-indicator signals taken alone.
