# Technical Analysis of Stock Trends — Robert D. Edwards & John Magee

The original classic source for Western chart-pattern theory (11th ed.).

## Head & Shoulders

**Top**: (A) a strong rally on very heavy volume climaxing an extended advance, then a minor recession on lower volume ("left shoulder"); (B) a second high-volume advance exceeding the left shoulder, then a reaction on lesser volume back below the left-shoulder top ("head"); (C) a third rally on decidedly less volume that fails to reach the head ("right shoulder"); (D) a decline through the neckline with a close beyond it by roughly 3% of the stock's price ("confirmation"). All four elements are needed. Measuring rule: project the head-to-neckline vertical distance down from the point of neckline penetration — the minimum probable objective, capped at the extent of the preceding advance. A "pullback" toward (rarely through) the neckline often follows the break.

**Bottom** ("Kilroy Bottom"): mirror of the top, with one key difference — the confirming upside breakout through the neckline **must** be accompanied by a volume burst (top breakdowns don't require volume confirmation, bottom breakouts do). Bottoms tend to take longer and be flatter than tops.

**Complex/multiple H&S**: shoulders or head doubled into multiple waves, with a strong tendency toward left/right symmetry (except in volume). The initial move out of a complex top is typically more gradual than a simple H&S's often-fast initial move.

## Rounding tops/bottoms

Gradual, symmetrical, curved reversals reflecting a slow supply/demand shift rather than a struggle. Rounding bottoms ("saucers") are common in low-priced stocks; volume declines steadily to a minimum at the exact bottom then re-expands with the upturn. Rounding tops are rarer in low/mid-priced stocks (which tend to top via H&S) and more common in high-grade, high-priced issues; volume stays high/irregular through the top. No standard measuring formula — extent is estimated from the size/duration of the rounding process.

## Triangles

**Symmetrical**: needs four established minor reversal points (2 tops, 2 bottoms) to draw both boundary lines. Roughly 3 of 4 are consolidations, not reversals. Volume diminishes toward the apex. The most reliable breakouts occur between roughly the halfway point and three-quarters of the horizontal distance to the apex — breakouts delayed further lose forecasting power. Downside breaks on unusually heavy volume right at the start are often false (a shakeout). Measuring: draw a line from the pattern's origin parallel to the opposite boundary; price is expected to reach it. Called the *least reliable* of the classic patterns.

**Right-angle (ascending/descending)**: a horizontal boundary (fixed-price supply or demand) plus a sloped boundary. These *do* give advance directional notice, unlike symmetrical triangles, and fail less often. Ascending = bullish (fixed supply gets absorbed); descending = bearish (fixed demand exhausts). Same volume/measuring rules as symmetrical triangles.

## Rectangles

A trading range bounded by roughly horizontal top and bottom lines — a picture of two evenly-matched groups fighting it out, rather than the "doubt" of a triangle. Volume diminishes as the rectangle lengthens. False breakouts are much less common than with triangles ("almost as reliable as an H&S, though not as powerful"), though premature breakouts are slightly more frequent. Measuring: minimum target is the pattern's own vertical height projected from the breakout; wide/active rectangles are more dynamic than long/narrow ones.

## Double/triple tops and bottoms

True double/triple tops and bottoms are stated to be "exceedingly rare" — the term is overused for what's really an early-stage ascending triangle or rectangle. A true double top needs the two tops separated by real time (more than roughly a month), an intervening reaction of ~15–20%, reduced volume on the second top, and a dull/rounding intervening low. Not confirmed until price breaks the intervening valley low (mirror rule for bottoms, breaking the intervening rally high).

## Flags and pennants

Short, compact consolidations after a fast, near-vertical move (the "mast"). A flag is a small parallelogram sloping moderately *against* the prevailing trend; a pennant is the same context but bounded by converging lines. Duration typically 5 days to 3 weeks. Reliability tests: must follow a genuine straight-line move; volume must diminish appreciably and continuously through construction; price must break away within about 4 weeks. Measuring ("half-mast"): project the distance of the preceding straight-line move from the point the flag/pennant began, from the breakout point, in the same direction — best measured on a semi-log scale.

## Gaps

Four meaningful types: **Common/Area** gaps (inside a congestion pattern, essentially no forecasting value beyond confirming a range is forming); **Breakaway** gaps (at the completion of a congestion pattern's breakout — confirm the move is genuine, since false moves rarely gap); **Continuation/Runaway/Measuring** gaps (mid-move, typically near the halfway point of a fast, straight-line move — price is expected to travel roughly as much further beyond the gap as it already traveled to reach it); **Exhaustion** gaps (at the very end of a fast move, on extreme volume, usually closed quickly within days — unlike runaway gaps, which stay open until a full reversal). An **Island Reversal** is a small trading range bounded by an exhaustion gap on one side and a breakaway gap on the other, signaling a full retracement of the immediately preceding move. The popular "a gap must be closed" rule is explicitly rejected as false.

## Support/resistance & trendlines

A support/resistance level's strength depends on three factors: (1) the volume that traded there originally (most important), (2) how far price has moved away from it before returning, and (3) how much time has elapsed. Broken resistance becomes support and vice versa. Trendline construction needs exactly two points; validity tests, in order of importance: number of touches (most important), length/duration, and angle (flatter lines are more significant than steep ones, which get broken by minor consolidations without real trend-change significance). A valid penetration needs a close beyond the line by roughly 3% of the stock's price. The "Fan Principle": when the third of a series of progressively flatter corrective trendlines (each drawn from the same origin) is broken, the correction's end is confirmed in the great majority of cases.

## Volume principles (cross-cutting)

Volume behaves systematically around every major pattern: rising into a genuine breakout, declining through a consolidation's formation, and spiking on genuine support/resistance penetrations. A 3% price-penetration test applies uniformly across neckline breaks, triangle/rectangle boundaries, double/triple-top valley levels, and trendlines.
